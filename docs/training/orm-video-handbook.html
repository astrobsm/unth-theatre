Part 0 — Production bible

Read this once. It makes all thirty-eight videos feel like one series instead of thirty-eight strangers.

How to use this handbook

Every video has the same four blocks. The brief says who it is for and what they will be able to do afterwards. The scene table is the shot list: what is on screen, what moves, and the exact words spoken over it. The narration is written to be spoken, not read — short sentences, no sub-clauses, no jargon a new porter would not recognise. The copy-paste prompt is a single condensed block to drop straight into an AI video generator.

Produce them in order within a part. Later videos assume the viewer has seen the earlier ones in that part, and every part assumes Part 1.

Cast, setting and visual language

ElementSpecification
PresenterOne recurring Nigerian presenter in theatre scrubs, mid-30s, warm and unhurried. The same person in every video, so the series has a face. To camera for the opening and closing only; narrating over the screen in between.
VoiceNigerian English, measured, about 130 words per minute. Confident, never patronising. This audience are clinicians. They are not being taught medicine, only where the buttons are.
SettingA Nigerian teaching-hospital theatre suite. Green and blue scrubs, tiled corridors, trolleys, a nurses' station with a laptop, staff using phones. Bright, clean, busy but not chaotic.
Device on screenA phone whenever the task is done standing up: rosters, milestones, check-in, calling for a patient. A laptop for desk work: booking, reports, publishing rosters. The app is mobile-first and the videos must look mobile-first.
Screen captureReal screens, not mock-ups. Zoom to 150% on the field being discussed. Highlight the tap target a beat before the finger arrives, so the eye is already there.
Patient namesAlways fictional; use the ones in these scripts. Never film a real folder number.
TitlesLower third, sans-serif, deep blue. Module name left, role right.
MusicSoft instrumental under the opening and closing only. Silent under instruction — the words are the content.

Rules that keep attention

The first eight seconds

Never open with "In this video we will look at…". Open with the problem the viewer has already had this week: "The patient was ready. The theatre was ready. Nobody had told the porter." Then the promise: "By the end of this video that will never happen on your list again."

  • One idea per sixty seconds. If two ideas are competing, cut it into two videos.
  • Show the consequence, not the feature. Not "tap Save", but "tap Save, and the pack provider's phone buzzes in the store tonight, while there is still time to find the mesh."
  • Name real people doing real jobs. Sister Ngozi. Dr Okeke. Emeka the porter. Abstractions do not hold attention; people do.
  • Say the cost of the shortcut. Every module has one tempting shortcut. Name it, and name what it costs, once, plainly.
  • Close with one action for the next working day — not a summary.
  • Stay under four minutes. Longer is watched once and never finished.

V2 · Getting onto the theatre Wi-Fi

All staff3 minBefore anything else

The brief

Nobody uses the app if they cannot get onto the network. This video is the single most-watched thing in the series, because every new member of staff needs it on their first morning. It must be slow, literal, and show a real phone.

They will be able to

  • Join the theatre Wi-Fi and get through the sign-in page unaided.
  • Understand that they sign in with their own ORM username and password — there is no shared Wi-Fi key.
  • Stay signed in for a full 24-hour shift, and walk between theatres without signing in again.
  • Know what to do when the sign-in page does not appear.

She has been trying for four minutes, and the case she needs to book is starting in twenty. This is the most common problem in the whole hospital, and it takes ninety seconds to solve for good.
2 · 0:18–0:50

Finger taps the notification. Open your Wi-Fi settings and pick the theatre network. There is no password on the network itself, so it will connect straight away. Now watch for this: a notification that says sign in to network. Tap it. On most phones it appears on its own within a few seconds. That notification is the door.
3 · 0:50–1:25
The portal		The captive portal page fills the screen: hospital name, a username field, a password field, one blue Log In button. Finger types a username, then a password shown as dots. Taps Log In. A brief spinner, then a success page. This is the captive portal. It is not a separate account. Type the same username and password you use for the ORM itself — your own name, your own password. That is deliberate. There is no shared Wi-Fi key in this hospital, because a shared key is a key that ends up written on a wall, and then anybody in the car park is on the theatre network. Your login is yours. Tap Log In, and you are through.
4 · 1:25–1:55
It lasts a shift		Clock graphic sweeping 24 hours. Phone in a pocket; the screen locks; hours pass; screen unlocks and the app opens instantly with no portal. Now the part that matters. Once you are in, you stay in for twenty-four hours. Your phone can lock, go in your pocket, run out of battery and come back — you do not sign in again. It used to be different. One of our anaesthetists signed in fourteen times in three days. That is fixed. One sign-in covers a full shift and the night after it.
5 · 1:55–2:25
Roaming		Overhead animation of the suite: a router icon, then several access points along the corridor joined by a line. A staff avatar walks from Theatre 1 to Theatre 4 and to recovery; the connection indicator stays solid green throughout. The signal comes from one router, and the access points along the corridors extend it. To your phone it is all one network. So you can walk from Theatre One to recovery, from recovery to the store, and the app keeps working. Your device is remembered for the day, so moving between theatres never puts the sign-in page back in front of you.

One: turn Wi-Fi off and on again, and wait five seconds. Two: open your browser and go to any plain address that is not secure — the portal cannot interrupt a secure page, which is why typing a search sometimes does nothing. Three: switch off mobile data while you sign in, because your phone will quietly prefer the mobile network and never ask the portal for the door. If none of those work, it is not you. Tell the theatre manager.

Close		The registrar from scene 1, now walking and booking on her phone, unhurried. One thing to do tomorrow: sign in once, at the start of your shift, before you need it. Then forget about it for the rest of the day.

V3 · Why the network is controlled

All staff2 min 30 sPairs with V2

The brief

Staff will discover that the theatre Wi-Fi is not an open internet cafe, and some will be annoyed. Better they hear the reason from us, framed around the patient, than invent a worse explanation. The tone is respectful and adult throughout — explaining, never scolding.

They will be able to

  • Explain why the network is metered and monitored, in terms of clinical priority.
  • Understand that their own account is tied to their activity, and what that means.
  • Know what the network is for, and what to use their own mobile data for.
  • Know that shared handsets do not mean shared accounts.

V4 · What follows you everywhere

All staff3 min

The brief

Five behaviours exist on every screen in the app. Teaching them once here saves explaining them in thirty-four later videos.

They will be able to

  • Understand the theatre radio, and why it ducks the music instead of stopping it.
  • Trust that a half-finished form survives switching apps.
  • Keep working when the internet drops, and know what happens to what they typed.
  • Find the Feedback button and the Role Guide.
SceneOn screenNarration
1 · 0:00–0:20
Hook		A nurse half-way through a long pre-op form. Her phone rings; she takes the call, opens WhatsApp to check a folder number, comes back eight minutes later. The form is exactly as she left it.
